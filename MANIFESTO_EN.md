# I wrote this with AI. But how do I say so?

*A small template, not a big idea.*

---

I have been writing code for a few years now, but never alone. AI
tools — Claude, ChatGPT, Abacus AI — are always in the loop. They
write when I ask, fix when I find errors, and we go back and forth
until something works. When I am done, I have a working thing in
front of me. But there is one question left:

**Who wrote it?**

Me? Them? Both of us?

There is no clean answer to this. License files have no field for
"AI was used." README acknowledgments feel awkward when the thing
you are thanking is a model. Putting "Built with Abacus AI" on the
About page feels like it does and does not say enough at the same
time. The software world has not yet built a habit for this.

## Why I made something

Because the gap was real. Working on my own project, I stopped one
day and asked: "Did I write this code?" No, the AI wrote it. "Then
what did I do?" I made the architecture, decided what to ask, said
no to wrong answers, tested, integrated. That is **something**, but
it is not "I wrote the code."

I thought this difference deserves a name. Since none existed, I
made a template. Its name: **ai-authorship-notice**.

## What the template does

A small markdown file with three sections:

1. **What AI tools I used**
2. **What decisions I (the human) made**
3. **This notice does not modify my license**

That's it. Not a legal document, not a contract. Just a
**statement** — an honest note about how the project was made. CC0
licensed, so anyone can take it, modify it, use it in their own
project.

On GitHub: github.com/hitsumo/ai-authorship-notice

## What the template cannot do

A lot, honestly:

- **It will not protect your code if someone steals it.** For that,
  git history and dated public releases matter more.
- **It carries no legal weight.** A statement is not a contract.
- **It does not settle any debate.** "Who owns AI-generated work" is
  still an open legal question.
- **It is not for everyone.** Closed-source commercial work, NDA
  projects, contracts with "no AI" clauses — **do not use it there**.

## What it might do

Maybe a few things:

- **Start a norm.** If enough people use it, "AI disclosure" might
  become a regular habit. Widely-used licenses went through a
  similar phase — no one forced them; they just became standard
  once they were common.
- **Force you to think.** Filling in the template makes you ask:
  "What did I actually do?" That is a good question.
- **Inform whoever uses your code.** Someone working on top of your
  project sees: this code was partly AI-generated, I should think
  about that when I make changes too.

## Maybe nothing happens

Maybe the template sits unused. Maybe a few projects pick it up,
then forget it. Maybe nobody cares.

That is fine. I go back to my computer, having lost nothing. But
trying felt better than not trying.

If you also work with AI and have wondered "how do I say this," the
template is there. Use it, do not use it, do something else
entirely. The choice is yours.

What I do want is this: **let's talk about it**. We do not yet have
a healthy way to separate human work from AI work, and finding that
way is not one person's job.
